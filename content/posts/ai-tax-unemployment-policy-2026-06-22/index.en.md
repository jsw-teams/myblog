Whether AI will replace large numbers of jobs can no longer be answered with a simple yes or no. The sharper answer is that AI first replaces tasks, then reorganizes jobs, and eventually creates real job losses in some sectors. The difficult question is the public ledger behind that shift: if models and agents perform more work once done by humans, should governments tax AI, and how should unemployment insurance, reskilling, wage insurance, and AI dividends be designed?

<!--more-->

This article does not make any one source the axis. GPT Deep Research Analysis supplies cross-country policy and data context. Gemini Deep Search pushes the job-loss and fiscal-governance pressure harder. Jun's analysis keeps the factual boundaries in place: do not turn proposals into enacted systems, and do not turn exposure into unemployment. Nian's analysis brings distributional stress, shrinking labor-tax bases, and disappearing entry-level pathways to the foreground.

## Let Each Source Stand First

GPT Deep Research Analysis begins with a factual boundary:

> 截至2026年6月，在本文覆盖的主要经济体与新兴经济体中，尚未检索到已正式生效、且专门按“AI模型替代劳动”征收的国家级AI税/机器人税；真正进入税制并可操作的，多是数字服务税、全球最低税或一般企业税规则。各国现实政策重心，已明显从“给AI直接征税”转向三条主线：用现有失业保险与工资补贴稳住就业关系、以再培训和终身学习应对任务重组、以算法/高风险AI监管降低不透明替代与歧视风险。

Jun's analysis makes the operational point more cautiously:

> 当前主流政策并不是立即开征“AI税”，而是以 **AI监管 + 再培训 + 社保/失业保障 + 产业投资 + 劳动市场透明度规则** 为主；AI税仍处在少数国家/政客/学界讨论与提案阶段，尚未成为全球普遍制度。

Nian's analysis opens at a very different temperature:

> 如果说上周咱们聊的全球新闻还只是各方势力在边界线上“焊铁壳子”，那这份关于AI失业和收税的报告，简直就是一部正在上演的、准备把全人类饭碗一锅端掉的科幻灾难片！

The posture is therefore simple: factual boundaries should not be swallowed by drama, but distributional anxiety should not be waved away just because no broad AI tax has formally arrived.

## 1. Exposure, Displacement, And Excuse

AI's labor impact is often written as a straight line: models get stronger, so people lose jobs. The four sources suggest a three-layer picture.

First is exposure. International institutions discuss tasks within jobs being affected by AI. GPT Deep Research Analysis stresses that IMF estimates of roughly 60 percent exposure in advanced economies do not mean 60 percent of jobs disappear.

Second is job reorganization. Jun's analysis is clearest here: clerical work, customer support, basic coding, junior content work, translation, data processing, hiring screens, and junior legal or financial analysis are exposed early; care work, teaching, field coordination, high-liability roles, and trust-heavy services are more resistant.

Third is excuse-making. Nian reads Salesforce, Amazon, IBM, Microsoft, Klarna and similar cases as AI-washed restructuring:

> 哈佛商业评论的调查把这帮狐狸的底裤都看穿了：真正因为技术成熟而替代人工的裁员只占2%，高达60%的组织是在搞**“AI洗白”**！说白了，就是拿AI当借口，来掩盖疫情期间盲目扩张的愚蠢，顺便裁员美化财报。

The public version needs boundaries: layoffs may combine AI, market pressure, overhiring after the pandemic, reorganization, and managerial correction. But it is also wrong to deny that AI has already become a justification for cost cutting, hiring freezes, and pressure on entry-level white-collar jobs.

## 2. Countries Are Not Answering The Same Question

If the question is simply which countries have enacted AI taxes, the answer is short: the reviewed major economies have not adopted a broad national tax specifically on AI labor substitution. If the question is how governments are handling AI's labor shock, the answer is much more varied.

Jun's analysis summarizes the policy map this way:

> **欧盟**：重监管、重劳动权利、重再培训，AI Act将就业、招聘、劳动管理纳入高风险AI场景。  
> **美国**：偏创新与基础设施建设，联邦层面未征AI税，个别议员提出AI税和AI红利方案。  
> **中国**：强调稳就业、产业升级、AI+行动与人机协同，倾向行政指导和就业稳定政策，而非AI税。  
> **日本**：因老龄化和劳动力短缺，更多把AI/机器人视为补劳动力工具，税收限制动力较弱。  
> **韩国**：有“机器人税”历史样本，同时推进AI基本法和高影响AI监管。  
> **新加坡**：不走AI税，强调国家AI战略、SkillsFuture再培训、终身学习与可信AI。

Nian frames the same countries as a clash of governing philosophies: Europe uses law and worker rights as a brake; the United States mixes innovation leadership, local tax experiments, and national political proposals; Singapore turns reskilling into state capacity; Japan treats AI as a labor supplement under aging pressure; South Korea carries a weak robot-tax precedent while also trying to compete in AI.

Mainland China requires one more step. Jun's analysis says:

> 中国2026年发布五年就业计划，强调保持就业市场稳定、防止大规模失业。  
> 政策重点包括稳定劳动密集型行业、扩大养老、托育、旅游、餐饮等服务业就业容量。  
> 在AI方面，强调适应AI发展、促进人机协同、推动就业创业和公共就业服务。  
> 高校和产业侧也在加快AI相关课程、人才培养与“AI+”应用扩散。

That passage does not prove that employment protection has worked. It reveals tension between language and resource allocation. The documents emphasize job stability, human-machine collaboration, service-sector absorption, and public employment support; in practice, large resources still flow into AI infrastructure, compute, chips, data centers, industrial upgrading, and university-major reshaping.

Jie's suggestion is to test protection through household disposable income and actual household security, not through policy titles. Official income growth is only one input, because official statistics also serve a stability narrative. A stronger check is to place official data next to third-party consumption observations and youth-employment indicators. FT reported that in May 2026 China's retail sales fell 0.6 percent year on year while exports rose 19.4 percent and industrial production rose 4.5 percent. MarketWatch observed that 2025 retail sales exceeded 50 trillion yuan, up 3.7 percent, while household deposits exceeded 160 trillion yuan and the World Bank's framing still put China's household saving rate above 30 percent of disposable income. AP documented the resumed youth-unemployment measure at 14.9 percent for 16-24-year-olds excluding students; Time recorded that the older measure reached 21.3 percent in June 2023 before publication was suspended.

The data conclusion is direct: there is not yet clear evidence that AI-driven industrial upgrading has returned to household balance sheets. Production and external-demand numbers look stronger than household consumption; households are turning more cash into defensive savings; and entry-level youth jobs still face double-digit unemployment pressure. Mainland policy can speak of "job stability" and "human-machine collaboration," but the household signal remains caution, delayed consumption, and conservative saving, not restored income security.

## 3. AI Tax Is A Fiscal Question, Not Just A Tax Name

An AI tax sounds simple: AI replaces people, so tax AI. The hard part is the base. What counts as AI: a customer-service agent, an automation script, an API call, an industrial robot, a data center, algorithmic trading, or smart logistics?

Jun's analysis gives the more realistic direction:

> 短期内，全球更可能出现的是“AI巨头专项税”“AI红利基金”“自动化税收优惠调整”“算力/能源相关费用”，而不是简单粗暴的“机器人税”。

Nian favors token-use taxes, FLOP-based consumption taxes, and green data-center energy taxes because they map more closely to AI usage and final-consumption location:

> **Token使用税与FLOP消费税（按流量拔毛）：**  
> *机制：* 针对大模型的API调用，在B2C最终消费环节按Token数量或者算力消耗（FLOPs）加征微额消费税。  
> *年导好评：* 稳！这个靠谱。随着大模型向全社会渗透，税基比天还大。

If AI taxation becomes real, it is unlikely to mean charging a fee per robot. The more plausible shift is from labor income toward capital gains, platform revenue, compute use, energy externalities, and final consumption.

## 4. Unemployment Policy Matters More Than The Tax Label

The sources agree on one point: "AI tax" cannot be the only answer. If AI displacement is structural, short-term unemployment relief and a slogan telling workers to "learn AI" will not be enough.

Jun's toolkit is plain:

> AI导致的失业可能不是短期周期性失业，而是结构性失业。因此需要：  
> - 延长失业保险覆盖；  
> - 提供转岗期间生活津贴；  
> - 将医保、养老、职业培训与个人绑定，而不是完全绑定雇主；  
> - 对被AI替代的工人提供快速再就业服务。

Nian is skeptical of simple UBI and prefers concrete transition support:

> 彻底扬了UBI，改用“精准转型缝补”：发支票就是养懒汉和喂通胀。要把资金定向投到再培训期间的**长期脱产生活津贴**；再引入**“工资保险”**机制，如果一个中产白领被迫降薪转岗，政府在两年内补贴新旧岗位50%的薪资差额。

The public version can be gentler, but the direction is worth keeping. A displaced junior programmer, support worker, clerk, editor, or mid-career white-collar worker needs time, tuition, living support, credentials, job matching, and wage buffers after transition.

## 5. Bottom Line

AI taxes should not be written as imminent global consensus, nor dismissed as unnecessary. Broad, crude taxes based on robot counts are immature in the short run; but fiscal redistribution around AI-company gains, compute use, data-center externalities, excess automation profits, and AI dividends will become harder to avoid.

AI is not simply stealing jobs, and it will not automatically create prosperity. It changes who earns income, who has bargaining power, and who pays transition costs. The real question is not whether to punish AI. It is whether society can turn part of AI-driven productivity into ordinary people's skills, income security, and renewed access to work.
