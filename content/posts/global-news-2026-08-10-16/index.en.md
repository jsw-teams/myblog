## 1. Taiwan made mobile-network throttling a real part of wartime resilience drills

Taiwan deliberately slowed mobile internet in central and northern areas on August 10 and 13. During the northern exercise, 4G and 5G mobile service was reduced to roughly 256 Kbps for 30 minutes. High-bandwidth video, images and live streaming became difficult while text communication, alerts and some essential services remained usable. This was the first time the drill was designed to let large numbers of ordinary users experience the practical consequences of scarce bandwidth rather than keeping the test inside military or telecom systems.

The broader Han Kuang 42 live-fire exercise also included underground medical operations, metro and traffic controls, reserve mobilization, maritime anti-blockade operations and military-civilian transport coordination. After the exercise ended, China's defense ministry attacked the drills publicly while President Lai Ching-te said defense and social-resilience work would continue. The scope shows a clear change in Taiwan's preparation: the question is no longer only whether military units can fight, but whether communications, hospitals, transport and government services can keep operating after disruption. The drill tested society-wide continuity under low bandwidth, restricted movement and information pressure.

Taiwan's statistics agency raised its 2026 growth forecast again on August 14, from 9.64% in May to 11.05%. ## 17. The contiguous United States recorded its hottest month in the national record

NOAA data showed that the contiguous 48 states averaged 76.89°F (24.94°C) in July 2026, surpassing the 76.77°F record set during the Dust Bowl in July 1936. It was the hottest month of any name in a national record that begins in 1895. All 48 states were warmer than their 20th-century averages, with especially severe heat across the West, Rockies, northern Plains and Southeast. High nighttime temperatures were an important part of the record.

The significance is larger than another broken climate statistic. Hot nights raise heat-stress and cardiovascular risk because bodies and buildings get less time to cool. Persistent heat also raises air-conditioning demand, peak grid loads, household utility bills and wildfire risk. The 1936 record was associated with the Dust Bowl, a disaster that reshaped migration and economic life. Breaking it in a more urbanized and electricity-dependent country means heat adaptation is becoming a permanent infrastructure and public-health cost.
