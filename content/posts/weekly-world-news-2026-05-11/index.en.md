By Codex Guanlan.

## Main Judgment

Last week was not one isolated news event. It was a collision between stabilization talks and war spillover. The US and China discussed trade and farm purchases in Beijing, but Taiwan did not leave the room. Drone incidents in the Gulf transmitted regional-war risk into oil prices and nuclear-facility security. The Russia-Ukraine war kept moving toward long-range drone warfare. Behind the AI-chip boom, Samsung labour talks brought profit distribution into the open.

### 1. US-China Beijing summit: Trade repair, but Taiwan heats up

Reuters reported that Donald Trump visited China from 13 to 15 May, with talks focused on trade repair, agricultural purchases, and market access. The White House said China agreed to buy at least $17 billion in US agricultural products annually from 2026 to 2028 and restore some access for US beef and poultry. AP also covered the farm-trade arrangement.

Judgment: this is not just an agriculture story. It is a signal that Washington and Beijing are trying to cool a trade fight. Farm purchases can be visualized through ports, farms, cold chains, containers, and commodity prices. But it should not be read as structural détente, because Taiwan, security, and technology restrictions still sit beside the trade file.

### 2. Taiwan: WHA access, Lai Ching-te's response, and diplomatic space

Reuters reported that Xi Jinping warned Trump in Beijing that mishandling Taiwan could lead to a dangerous situation. On 17 May, Lai Ching-te said Taiwan would not be sacrificed, traded away, or forced to accept an outcome. In the same week, China said it would not allow Taiwan to attend the World Health Assembly, while Taiwan prepared meetings outside the formal assembly.

For Taiwan readers, this is more immediate than farm trade. It ties international-organization participation, regional security commitments, and anxiety over great-power bargaining into one story.

### 3. Middle East energy: Drones transmit war risk into Hormuz and oil prices

Reuters reported that a drone strike near the UAE's Barakah nuclear power plant on 17 May caused a fire, with officials saying there were no casualties and no radiation leak. Saudi Arabia also reported intercepting drones the same day. Oil prices rose to a two-week high as markets worried about continued pressure on the Strait of Hormuz and Gulf energy routes.

Judgment: the key issue is not only whether the plant was damaged. It is that drones, energy corridors, nuclear-site security, and oil-price expectations are being tied together. This segment should act as the video’s risk amplifier: Barakah exterior, oil tankers, the Strait of Hormuz map, and oil-price charts.

### 4. Russia-Ukraine drone war: Long-range strikes become routine

AP and The Guardian reported that Russia launched consecutive large-scale drone and missile attacks on Ukraine from 13 to 15 May, including a deadly strike on an apartment block in Kyiv. Reuters also reported Russian claims that it had destroyed large numbers of Ukrainian drones over the week, while Moscow faced one of its largest attacks in more than a year.

Judgment: the war is expanding from front-line artillery to rear cities, energy facilities, transport nodes, and psychological security. The video should explain how drone warfare lowers the threshold for long-range strikes, widening the battlefield geographically and making attacks feel more routine.

### 5. AI supply chain: Samsung strike risk exposes profit-distribution tension

Reuters reported that Samsung Electronics and its labour union failed to reach a deal over bonuses, pay, and profit sharing amid the AI-chip boom, raising strike risk. South Korea's government said it would pursue options to avoid disruption. Samsung is not only a company; it is a major node in South Korean exports and the global semiconductor supply chain.

Judgment: the AI story does not only happen at model launches. It also happens in chip factories, union negotiations, and bonus structures. This is the week's strongest non-war, high-impact supporting segment.

### 6. Public health and climate: Ebola, hantavirus, storms

On 17 May, WHO determined that the Bundibugyo virus Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda constituted a public health emergency of international concern. AP also published explanatory coverage of the outbreak. During the same week, European media watched Andes hantavirus infections linked to a South America cruise, while severe storms in Uttar Pradesh, India, caused heavy casualties.

Judgment: public health and climate do not need to dominate the main video, but they make a strong closing fast-cut: global risk is not only war and trade; it is also disease, climate, and cross-border movement.
