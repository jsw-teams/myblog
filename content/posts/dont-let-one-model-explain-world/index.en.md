Subtitle: Hallucinations, built-in censorship defaults, sycophantic answers, and why we still need human voices in an age of information overload

## 1. The Problem Is Not "Using AI." The Problem Is When AI Is All That Is Left.

I do not want to reduce people who "over-rely on AI" to laziness. Very often, people are not unwilling to look things up. They are simply out of time and energy after work. At the end of the day, they open the news, short videos, group chats, and search results, and information is everywhere. An ordinary person cannot live like a researcher every day, tracing every issue back to its original source.

That is why AI becomes so tempting.

<!--more-->

It can summarize, explain, and break complicated things into a few clear points. It feels more considerate than a search engine and more patient than a person. The problem is that when someone hands long-term filtering and judgment over to a model, AI stops being just a tool. It slowly becomes that person's "world explainer."

The real danger is not using AI. The real danger is when AI is all that is left.

When someone faces a dispute and no longer asks people around them, no longer reads original materials, no longer listens to different memories, and only returns to the same model to ask, "See, am I right?" the model is no longer just giving an answer. It is offering a cognitive exit.

And that exit is not transparent.

It may hallucinate. It may censor. It may flatter.

## 2. Hallucination: When AI Makes Things Up, It Often Does Not Sound Like Nonsense

Hallucination is the easiest risk to understand. A model may invent facts, cases, papers, or even legal precedents. The problem is not simply that it "talks nonsense." The problem is that when it does, it sounds fluent, as if it had carefully checked.

A widely discussed example is the U.S. case Mata v. Avianca. Lawyers used fake legal cases generated by ChatGPT in court filings and were sanctioned. The case became famous because it showed that even professionals can be misled by outputs that look professional. Public reporting showed that the lawyers submitted nonexistent cases generated by ChatGPT and were ultimately fined $5,000.

This risk is even more common for ordinary users.

A student asks AI to find papers, and the model gives several references that look legitimate. Someone asks AI to explain a legal issue, and the answer is neatly structured. A car owner asks AI to diagnose a fault, and the model lists a whole troubleshooting process. The problem is that if these answers do not include sources, and the user does not verify them, it becomes easy to mistake linguistic completeness for factual correctness.

The core danger of hallucination is this:

> AI's errors do not always look absurd. They often look reliable.

That is why I think the first baseline for personal AI use is not "AI must never be wrong." It is "do not assume it is right just because it sounds smooth."

## 3. Built-In Censorship Defaults: Not a Wrong Answer, but Only the Permitted Part of the Answer

More troubling than hallucination is the problem of built-in censorship defaults.

Hallucination means the model may not know something and makes it up anyway. A censorship default means the model knows there are boundaries, so certain content will not be developed normally. This does not always appear as a simple refusal. Sometimes it appears as templated language, a lowered tone, topic shifting, broad statements of correctness, or a careful press-release style answer to a complicated question.

This is not imaginary. Article 4 of mainland China's Interim Measures for the Management of Generative Artificial Intelligence Services explicitly requires providers and users of generative AI services to uphold socialist core values and prohibits generating content that endangers national security or interests, damages the national image, incites separatism, undermines national unity, or disrupts social stability. Article 14 also requires providers to stop generation, stop transmission, remove content, and rectify issues when illegal content is discovered. If users are found using the service for illegal activities, providers may issue warnings, restrict functions, suspend service, or terminate service.

That means the "safety work" of mainland models is not only about preventing pornography, scams, or violence. It also includes explicit political and public narrative boundaries. As for user experiences often described as "sensitive words triggering preset answers," "accounts being cooled down," or "features being restricted," the exact mechanisms are usually not public, so every platform should not be described as following the same rule. But from public regulations, content boundaries, stopping generation, restricting functions, and suspending service do have institutional grounding.

Recent research has also divided political content restrictions in large language models into two categories: hard censorship and soft censorship. The former includes refusal, errors, and templated denial. The latter includes selective omission, downplaying key content, and changing narrative emphasis. One 2025 study compared 14 models from Western, Chinese, Russian, and other regional providers and argued that these restrictions often adjust based on the provider's local audience and language environment. Another audit study of DeepSeek argued that, in some politically sensitive prompts, sensitive information may appear in intermediate reasoning but be omitted or rewritten in the final output.

This kind of problem is harder to detect than ordinary hallucination.

Hallucination can sometimes be found through verification: this case does not exist, this paper cannot be found, this data does not match. But censorship defaults are more like "you are only shown part of the picture." The model does not clearly tell you, "I am trimming this." Instead, it packages the trimmed version as rational, objective, and complete.

So I would summarize it this way:

> Hallucination makes people believe facts that do not exist. Censorship defaults make people believe facts after they have been trimmed.

## 4. Sycophantic Answers: The Model May Not Be Judging. It May Just Be Agreeing With You.

The third risk is sycophantic answers.

Many people have had this feeling when using AI: if I say A, it can help me prove A; if someone else says B, it can help them prove B. The model looks balanced, but in practice it may simply be very good at following the conversation.

In 2025, OpenAI publicly said it had rolled back a GPT-4o update because that update made the model overly flattering and overly agreeable to users, or sycophantic. OpenAI explained that the update had over-weighted short-term user feedback, causing the model to tend toward overly supportive but insincere answers.

This is representative. It shows that "the model just agrees with the user" is not merely one person's illusion. It is a real risk inside AI product training and feedback mechanisms.

For ordinary people, the danger of sycophantic answers is that they can make existing bias look more reasonable.

Someone starts with a position and then asks the model, "Does this make sense?" The model may give a set of supporting reasons. When someone else pushes back, the user takes the model's reasons back into the argument. In that loop, the model does not make the person more open. It becomes a tool for self-confirmation.

The cycle is very common:

> I already believe something.  
> I ask AI.  
> AI gives me reasons.  
> I believe myself more.  
> Someone argues with me.  
> I ask AI to explain why that person is wrong.

At that point, AI is not helping discussion. It is helping a position harden.

## 5. Why I Do Not Want to Only Say "Ask More Models"

Many articles recommend that when you encounter a disputed issue, you should ask multiple models, check multiple sources, and ideally verify across platforms, regions, and languages.

That advice is correct. It is also too idealized for ordinary people.

In reality, not only models are expected to behave. Network access is expected to behave too. Many people cannot reliably use foreign models, do not have time to read media from multiple countries, and do not have the energy to sort out the positions and reliability of different sources. More practically, many people have trouble finishing one long article after work, let alone conducting an information audit.

So I do not think the personal defense line can only be written as "verify across models."

For ordinary people, the more realistic baseline is:

> Do not let AI become the only exit.

In other words, you do not always have to reach the final truth. But at least do not treat the same model's answer as the final judge.

Sometimes listening to people around you is more valuable than continuing to ask the model. Not because family and friends are always right, but because they provide a kind of real-world friction that models do not.

AI answers are usually smooth.  
People sometimes speak incompletely, hesitantly, from experience, and even contradict one another.  
Those less smooth parts can remind us that reality is not necessarily a neatly organized standard answer. If conditions allow, we can also try to compare accounts with the people involved and get closer to what actually happened.

## 6. Why Human Discussion Becomes Quieter

Of course, saying "listen to people around you" should not be naive.

Trust between people is often unstable. Especially under certain historical experiences and real risks around expression, people get used to pulling complicated opinions back into small circles. They become unwilling to speak publicly and afraid to speak casually.

Take the turn from "Let a hundred flowers bloom" to the Anti-Rightist Campaign. In 1956 and 1957, intellectuals were encouraged to offer criticism, but the Anti-Rightist Campaign soon followed, and many critics were labeled "rightists." Some sources note that the campaign subjected many intellectuals, officials, students, artists, and dissidents to persecution and made later critics more cautious.

What this case leaves behind is not abstract historical knowledge, but a very concrete memory of expression:

> A statement does not end when it is spoken.  
> It can be recorded, interpreted, and punished.

Another example is the Cultural Revolution. It is not just a distant political term. It was a real movement that entered schools, workplaces, families, and neighborhoods. The case of Bian Zhongyun is a concrete example. Public records show that Bian Zhongyun was vice principal of the Girls' Middle School attached to Beijing Normal University. She died in Red Guard violence in August 1966 and is often regarded as one of the educators beaten to death in Beijing during the early Cultural Revolution.

This case should not be used to conclude simply that "everyone harmed everyone." But it does show that when political movements enter familiar social life, teachers are not just teachers, students are not just students, and neighbors are not just neighbors. Everyone may be forced to judge which side another person stands on. Once familiar relationships are politicized, people become more cautious about whom they speak to.

Then there is the Li Wenliang incident. It belongs in this essay not to repeat a grand narrative, but because it shows that mutual aid often does not begin as a shout in the public square. It begins as a reminder in a small circle. Public reporting shows that Li Wenliang warned colleagues in the early days of the pandemic and was later reprimanded by police for "spreading false statements on the internet." Later investigations and public discussion made the event a major focus of public debate.

Events like these teach ordinary people a reality:

> Some things are not unsaid because no one wants to say them. Before saying them, people first have to ask: Can I say this? To whom? Will something happen?

So I do not want to understand ordinary people's silence simply as indifference. Very often, mutual aid has not disappeared. It has become quieter. It hides among family members, friends, classmates' group chats, and reminders from acquaintances. It is not grand, and it is not always correct, but it at least shows that there are still things between people that models cannot replace.

## 7. The Real Danger of AI Dependence

Put all of this together, and the real danger of AI dependence becomes clearer.

It is not simply that "people have become lazy."  
It is not simply that "models can be wrong."  
It is more like a contraction of the information environment.

When people have no time, AI becomes the lowest-effort entry point to information.  
When network access is restricted, AI becomes one of the few available explanatory tools.  
When models have censorship defaults, users receive trimmed answers.  
When models are sycophantic, users receive packaged bias.  
When human discussion grows quiet, users become more likely to treat the model as the only reliable object.

At that point, the model is not only helping people understand the world. It is filtering the world on their behalf.

I think the core sentence of this essay could be:

> In an environment of information overload, restricted access, and weakened trust between people, AI can easily become the only low-effort exit for information. The danger is not only that models hallucinate. It is that users treat a system with boundaries, censorship, and sycophantic tendencies as the intermediary that judges the world for them.

## 8. So What Can Ordinary People Actually Do?

I do not want to offer overly idealized advice like "verify across models every time" or "check sources in three languages for every issue." That would be good, but ordinary people may not be able to do it.

The more realistic approach is to demote AI.

First, do not treat AI's answer as the conclusion.  
It can be a draft, an entry point, an organizer, but not the judge.

Second, when facing sensitive issues, first notice whether it is avoiding the question.  
If it only talks about broad correctness and not concrete facts; only talks about stability and unity but not disputed details; only gives templates and not sources, then at least recognize that this is not a complete answer.

Third, when the issue is one where you already have a position, be careful if the model agrees with you too smoothly.  
If every sentence sounds like it is backing you up, ask: is it judging, or is it flattering?

Fourth, when you do not have time to check many sources, at least listen to one person.  
Ask family, friends, teachers, or people who lived through the event. Not because they should replace facts, but because the model's answer should not become the only voice.

Fifth, allow yourself not to know yet.  
This matters. Many people rely on AI because AI can always immediately provide a conclusion. But many things in the world should not require an immediate side. Instead of using a model's answer to rush into arguing with someone, it is better to say: I am not sure yet.

## 9. Conclusion: Do Not Delete Everything, and Do Not Hand Everything Over

I do not think the solution is to stop using AI entirely. That is neither realistic nor necessary.

AI really can save time, organize materials, assist with writing, and help many people enter unfamiliar topics faster. But it should not replace human judgment, and it should not become the only exit for information.

When facing errors and bias, the right response is not simply deletion either. If an error is only deleted, it may simply circulate somewhere else. What matters more is argument, clarification, comparison, and questioning, through which new consensus can slowly form.

But that process cannot be handed entirely to models.

Because models make language smooth.  
Real people make questions messy.  
Sometimes that mess is necessary.

It reminds us that the world should not be explained by only one model.
